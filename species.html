Section 02 — Flora & Fauna

Characteristic Species of Summit Lake Park

Summit Lake Park is one of the most biologically significant ecosystems in North America — a disjunct arctic tundra whose species are more typical of northern Alaska than the rest of the Rockies. Many are endemic to this one location.

A Disjunct Ecosystem

Summit Lake Park is what ecologists call a disjunct ecosystem — a pocket of arctic tundra that exists thousands of miles outside of the Arctic Circle. This isolation makes the community extraordinarily fragile. The species found here have evolved in conditions of extreme cold, intense UV radiation, thin soils, and a growing season that lasts only from July to August. That roughly 60-day window is all these plants have to grow, reproduce, and store energy for the long winter ahead.

When visitors stray off trail and trample vegetation, the consequences can be severe. Crushed plants may not have the energy reserves to repair themselves before dormancy sets in — and in some cases, they may never recover. The community here took thousands of years to develop, and it cannot be replaced on any human timescale.

Characteristic Flora

The plants of Summit Lake Park have developed remarkable adaptations to survive where almost nothing else can. The following species are considered iconic representatives of this alpine community.

Saxifraga foliolosa

Leafy Saxifrage

One of the rarest plants in the contiguous U.S. Rather than relying on seeds, this small forb reproduces via tiny plantlets called bulbils — an adaptation to the incredibly short growing season, where there is often not enough time to complete a full seed cycle.

Among Rarest in Lower 48
Phippsia algida

Ice Grass

A tiny grass that thrives in the meltwater zones of snowbanks. It is typically found on Arctic islands and its presence at Summit Lake is a testament to the park's exceptionally high-arctic character, far south of its typical range.

Arctic Disjunct
Draba grayana

Gray's Peak Whitlow-grass

A small, yellow-flowered plant that grows in rocky scree slopes. Found only on a handful of high peaks in Colorado, making it one of the most geographically restricted wildflowers in the state.

Colorado Endemic
Claytonia megarhiza

Alpine Spring Beauty

Distinctive for its large, succulent leaves and a deep taproot that anchors it firmly in shifting talus. The deep root also serves as an energy reservoir, allowing the plant to bloom rapidly as soon as snowmelt permits.

Talus Specialist
Bistorta vivipara

Alpine Bistort

A common but ecologically vital tundra resident. Like the Leafy Saxifrage, it produces starchy bulbils rather than relying entirely on seeds — these bulbils are an important food source for local wildlife including pikas and ground squirrels.

Keystone Forb

Characteristic Fauna

The animals of Summit Lake Park are equally extraordinary. Many are highly adapted to the severe conditions above treeline, relying on behavioral strategies, physiological adaptations, or both to survive.

Oreamnos americanus

Mountain Goat

Though not native to Colorado — they were introduced in the 1940s–60s — mountain goats are the most visible large mammal at Summit Lake. They are highly adapted to the steep cliff faces surrounding the cirque and are a defining visual feature of the park.

Non-Native / Introduced
Ovis canadensis

Rocky Mountain Bighorn Sheep

Native to the region and often seen on the "Summit Lake Flats," where they compete with introduced mountain goats for grazing space. Their presence reflects the park's role as critical high-elevation wildlife habitat.

Native — Resident
Marmota flaviventris

Yellow-bellied Marmot

Often heard before they are seen, marmots produce a sharp whistle as an alarm call from the boulder fields. They spend up to 8 months of the year in hibernation — one of the longest hibernation periods of any North American mammal.

Stable
Ochotona princeps

American Pika

Small, rabbit-like mammals that do not hibernate. Instead, they spend the brief summer frantically creating "haypiles" — caches of dried plants stored under boulders to sustain them through winter. Cannot survive prolonged exposure to temperatures above ~75°F, making them one of the most climate-threatened mammals in the West.

Climate Threatened
Lagopus leucura

White-tailed Ptarmigan

A master of camouflage: pure white in winter to blend with snow, and mottled brown in summer to match tundra rocks. Colorado's only year-round alpine bird, and a symbol of the alpine tundra ecosystem.

Alpine Specialist

Threats to Wildlife

These plants and animals face unique and compounding challenges. Chief among them are trampling from visitors, degrading soils, and warmer summer temperatures driven by climate change.

The growing season at Summit Lake lasts only from July to August — roughly 60 days. Plant life forms the trophic foundation of this entire ecosystem, and it has precious little time to maximize growth before entering dormancy for the majority of the year. When plants are crushed by human feet, they may not have the energy reserves to repair themselves and survive to the following summer. The cascading effects move up the food chain: less plant material means less food for pikas, marmots, and bighorn sheep; fewer prey animals means less food for raptors and predators that depend on this habitat.

Visitor Impact

A single footstep off-trail can crush cushion plants that took decades to grow. In the short alpine growing season, damaged plants often cannot regenerate before winter — and some never recover at all.

Ecological Note

The species at Summit Lake Park are not isolated curiosities — they form a functional community shaped by thousands of years of co-evolution. Losing any one element can cascade through the food web and alter the ecosystem in ways that are difficult or impossible to reverse.
