Section 02 — Flora & Fauna

Characteristic Species of Summit Lake Park

The alpine tundra of Summit Lake Park is home to a remarkable community of plants and animals. Each species has evolved extraordinary adaptations to survive above treeline — and each faces real threats in a changing world.

Alpine Flora

Plant life in the alpine tundra must cope with a growing season of fewer than 60 days, temperatures that can drop below freezing on any night of the year, powerful desiccating winds, and intense solar radiation. The flora that thrives here has evolved over thousands of years to meet these challenges.

Silene acaulis

Moss Campion

A classic cushion plant that forms dense, hemispherical mats just inches above the ground to avoid wind and retain warmth. Individual mats can be centuries old. Highly sensitive to trampling.

Species of Concern
Bistorta bistortoides

American Bistort

One of the most abundant and ecologically important wildflowers of the alpine tundra. Its white flower spikes are a critical nectar source for early-season pollinators and an important food for rodents and bears.

Stable
Ranunculus adoneus

Alpine Buttercup

One of the first plants to bloom after snowmelt, often pushing through the snow itself. A key indicator species for snowmelt timing — shifts in bloom date signal climate change impacts.

Phenology Shifts
Kobresia myosuroides

Bellardi Bog Sedge

The dominant plant of dry alpine tundra meadows. Forms a dense, turf-like community with extremely slow recovery from disturbance — footsteps can leave lasting damage for decades.

Disturbance Vulnerable
Caltha leptosepala

Marsh Marigold

Found in wet areas near snowfields and streams, this early bloomer is a critical resource for native bees and flies emerging in early summer. Highly dependent on stable snowpack timing.

Climate Sensitive
Salix planifolia

Planeleaf Willow

A low-growing shrubby willow that forms dense thickets in moist depressions. Provides nesting habitat for white-crowned sparrows and cover for small mammals. Expanding in range with warming temperatures.

Expanding Range

Alpine Fauna

Animals of the alpine tundra are equally specialized. Many spend their entire lives above treeline; others migrate seasonally. All are dependent on specific habitat features that disturbance can rapidly degrade.

Ochotona princeps

American Pika

A small, charismatic relative of rabbits that lives among talus slopes. Cannot thermoregulate in warm temperatures and faces habitat loss as warming pushes its range to higher, ultimately unavailable elevations.

Climate Threatened
Lagopus leucura

White-tailed Ptarmigan

Colorado's only year-round alpine bird, with plumage that changes from brown in summer to white in winter. Highly sensitive to disturbance during breeding season (June–July). Vulnerable to climate-driven vegetation shifts.

Species of Concern
Anaxyrus boreas

Western Toad

Breeds in the shallow margins of Summit Lake. Populations have declined dramatically across the West due to the chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) and habitat loss. Present but imperiled at Summit Lake.

Imperiled
Marmota flaviventris

Yellow-bellied Marmot

A keystone species of the talus community. Hibernates 7–8 months per year. Marmot colonies are important prey for eagles and hawks and their burrows create habitat structure for other species.

Stable
Aquila chrysaetos

Golden Eagle

A top predator that hunts over the open tundra of Summit Lake Park. Nests on cliff faces of Mount Evans. Territorial during nesting (Feb–July) and sensitive to disturbance near nest sites.

Disturbance Sensitive
Erebia callias

Colorado Alpine Butterfly

A specialist butterfly restricted to alpine tundra above 11,000 feet in Colorado. Adults fly for only 2–3 weeks per year. One of the most range-restricted butterflies in North America.

Range Restricted

Invasive Species Threats

While the harsh alpine climate historically acted as a barrier to most invasive species, warming temperatures and increased human traffic are now enabling several problematic non-native plants to establish footholds in and around Summit Lake Park.

Poa pratensis

Kentucky Bluegrass

Spreads aggressively along trails and disturbed areas, displacing native cushion plants and sedges. Thrives on disturbed, compacted soils created by off-trail foot traffic.

Invasive — Spreading
Cirsium arvense

Canada Thistle

Spreading upslope from lower-elevation roadsides, this persistent invader is now appearing in disturbed areas near the Summit Lake trailhead and parking areas.

Invasive — Monitoring
Ecological Note

The species documented at Summit Lake Park are not isolated curiosities — they form a functional community. Losing any one species can cascade through the food web and alter ecosystem processes. Conservation must take a whole-community approach.
