Section 01 — Why It Matters

The Importance of Summit Lake Park

Summit Lake Park is not merely a scenic destination — it is a critical ecological node, a regional water source, and a living laboratory for understanding alpine systems under climate stress.

Ecological Significance

Perched at 12,830 feet on the western flank of Mount Evans, Summit Lake Park occupies the alpine tundra zone — an ecosystem type that covers less than 3% of Colorado's land area yet supports a disproportionate share of the state's biodiversity. Alpine tundra habitats are characterized by their extreme environmental conditions: temperatures below freezing for much of the year, intense UV radiation, persistent winds, and a growing season that spans only 6–8 weeks in summer.

These harsh conditions have driven the evolution of highly specialized plant and animal communities found nowhere else on Earth at lower elevations. The biodiversity concentrated in Summit Lake Park represents an irreplaceable ecological heritage — one that cannot be recreated if lost.

Key Fact

Alpine tundra ecosystems store an estimated 3–5× more carbon per acre in soil than temperate forests, making their preservation essential to regional climate resilience.

Hydrological Importance

Summit Lake itself is fed by snowmelt from the surrounding high ridges and serves as an important headwater for the Bear Creek watershed, which eventually drains into the South Platte River — the primary water supply for the Denver metropolitan area. The lake's cold, clear water supports cold-adapted invertebrate communities and provides critical breeding habitat for several amphibian species.

Alpine wetlands surrounding the lake act as natural sponges, absorbing spring snowmelt and releasing it slowly through summer. This hydrological buffering capacity is especially important during drought years. Degradation of the lake's watershed — through trampling of vegetated banks, soil compaction, or introduction of pollutants — can disrupt water quality for communities far downstream.

Watershed Connectivity

Summit Lake Park sits at the top of a connected ecological corridor that links high-elevation wilderness with lower montane forests and riparian corridors. This corridor allows species to move seasonally and in response to climatic shifts. Maintaining the ecological integrity of the park protects not only the species that live there year-round, but also the many others that depend on it as a seasonal refuge.

Climate Sensitivity

Alpine tundra is among the most climate-sensitive ecosystems in the world. Research from the Mountain Research Station at Niwot Ridge (approximately 25 miles north of Summit Lake) has documented significant changes over the past three decades: earlier snowmelt dates, longer frost-free seasons, upward migration of treeline, and shifts in the timing and composition of wildflower blooms.

These changes are already detectable at Summit Lake Park. Warmer summers have allowed shrubby willows and woody plants to encroach on areas previously dominated by cushion plants and sedge meadows. As the climate continues to warm, many alpine specialists face a shrinking habitat with nowhere higher to migrate.

Threat Level

Colorado's alpine tundra is projected to lose up to 40–70% of its current extent by 2100 under high-emissions climate scenarios. Summit Lake Park represents one of the last large, relatively intact examples of this ecosystem in the Front Range.

Cultural and Recreational Value

Summit Lake Park is one of the most accessible pieces of true alpine wilderness in the continental United States. Located just 45 miles from downtown Denver via the Mount Evans Scenic Byway — the highest paved road in North America — the park receives tens of thousands of visitors annually. For many Front Range residents, it is their first and only encounter with alpine tundra.

This accessibility is both a tremendous asset and a significant challenge. The park serves an important educational function, connecting urban populations with wild landscapes. At the same time, it makes the ecosystem vulnerable to overuse, vandalism, and degradation of the very qualities that make it special.

Research Value

Summit Lake Park has been the site of scientific research by Colorado State University, the University of Colorado, and the Denver Museum of Nature and Science. Its long-elevation gradient, relatively undisturbed soils, and documented history make it an important reference site for understanding how alpine ecosystems respond to environmental change. Protecting the park protects an irreplaceable scientific resource.

Summary

Summit Lake Park matters because it is rare, fragile, functionally important, and under growing threat. Its conservation is an ecological imperative — and an opportunity to demonstrate what responsible stewardship of public lands looks like.
